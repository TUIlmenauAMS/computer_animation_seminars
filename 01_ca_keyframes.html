1. Keyframes & Interpolation

In traditional animation, a senior animator draws the key poses of a character — the frames that matter most — and an assistant fills in all the frames between them. Computer animation works the same way: the animator specifies values at key moments in time, and the software computes every value in between. Those specified values are called keyframes.

The simplest way to fill in the gaps is linear interpolation: given the value at keyframe i and the value at keyframe i+1, assume a constant rate of change and move smoothly from one to the other. This is mathematically clean, but it produces motion that often looks mechanical — we will address that in Demo 2.

The step interpolation mode holds the keyframe value constant until the next keyframe is reached, with no transition at all. It looks jarring for continuous motion, but it is the right choice for discrete properties like sprite frames or state machines.

Core idea: A keyframe stores the value of a property at a specific point in time. The animator sets the keyframes; the computer fills in the frames between them — this is called inbetweening or interpolation. Linear interpolation is the simplest: it assumes constant velocity between keyframes.

3. How It Works

The script is divided into nine numbered steps. Steps 1–5 are standard WebGL setup: context, shaders, helpers, compile/link, and geometry buffers. The animation concepts live in Steps 6–8.

Step 6 defines the keyframe track — a plain JavaScript array of objects, each holding a time, a position, and a color. This is the data structure that drives everything. Step 7 wires the UI controls to the playback state. Step 8 is the render loop: it accumulates time, looks up the two surrounding keyframes, computes the local t value, and interpolates both position and color before sending them to the GPU as uniforms.

The small discs on the ground are the five keyframe positions, drawn with gl.POINTS. They never move — they are the raw data. The sphere moves between them.

Note on color interpolation: This demo interpolates color in RGB space using the same linear lerp as position. RGB lerp is not perceptually uniform — the perceived brightness can dip in the middle of a blend. Professional tools such as Blender and After Effects offer interpolation in HSL or Oklab (a perceptually uniform color space) to produce more natural-looking color transitions. RGB is used here for simplicity and to keep the focus on the keyframe concept.
