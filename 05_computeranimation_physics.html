Chapter 05: Physics-based Animation

Keyframe and skeletal animation give artists direct control over every pose — but the real world is governed by forces, mass, and collision. Physics-based animation lets the simulation compute motion that would take thousands of keyframes to replicate by hand: a stack of boxes toppling under gravity, a chain of bodies swinging from a fixed anchor, a character's coat following through on a sharp turn. The motion emerges from initial conditions and physical laws, not from an artist's deliberate specification of every intermediate state.

This chapter introduces two physics engines. In the browser: Rapier — a modern, WASM-compiled rigid body engine written in Rust, designed to run at real-time frame rates alongside Three.js. In Python: PyBullet — a widely used open-source physics SDK used in robotics research, reinforcement learning, and game prototyping, and compatible with both local machines and headless environments such as Google Colab and GitHub Codespaces. Both engines share the same conceptual core: a world of rigid bodies, each with mass and velocity, stepped forward in discrete time by resolving forces and collisions.

By the end of this chapter you will understand the physics loop and why it uses a fixed timestep, be able to build a Rapier scene with rigid bodies, colliders, joints, and spring-damper secondary animation integrated into a Three.js render loop, and write a PyBullet simulation that records motion data to a NumPy array for playback or export.

1. Keyframe vs Simulation: When to Use Each

1.1 The two approaches

The history of computer animation is largely the story of two complementary strategies for producing motion:

Keyframe animation — an artist explicitly specifies the value of every animated property at specific points in time. The runtime interpolates between those keys. This gives full artistic control: the motion is exactly what the animator intended, it is reproducible frame-for-frame, and it is deterministic. The cost is labour — producing convincing secondary motion (a loose ponytail, a swinging bag, a character's coat reacting to a sudden stop) by hand requires hundreds of additional keyframes and significant skill.

Simulation-based animation — initial conditions (position, velocity, mass, forces) are given to a physics engine, which steps the world forward in time by integrating equations of motion and resolving collisions. The motion emerges from the simulation. No artist needs to key every frame. The trade-off is control: the simulation produces physically plausible motion, but compelling and physically plausible are not the same thing. Achieving a specific dramatic pose requires carefully tuning initial conditions or overriding the simulation.

1.2 When to use each

ApproachBest forNot ideal for
Keyframe Hero character performance, facial animation, story-critical motion, cutscenes Hundreds of interacting objects, cloth, fluids, secondary motion on accessories
Physics simulation Rigid body stacks, ragdoll death, cloth, secondary jiggle, environmental destruction Precise choreography, lip sync, shot-critical poses
Combined A character's skeleton is keyframed; a coat, hair, or held object is simulated relative to it —

1.3 Combining the two: driven + simulated motion

The professional pattern is driven + simulated: keyframes drive the primary skeleton — the artist controls the performance — while physics simulation drives secondary elements such as a ponytail, a cape, or loose accessories. The physics engine receives the animated bone positions as kinematic inputs and simulates the secondary objects relative to them. The secondary motion is physically coherent with the primary animation without the animator touching it.

Unreal Engine's Chaos physics, Unity's PhysX integration, and Blender's rigid body cache all implement this hybrid model. Demo 4 in this chapter implements the same pattern using a spring-damper system in pure JavaScript.

Physics is not a replacement for artistry. A physics simulation produces physically plausible motion — but "correct" and "compelling" are not the same thing. Professional animators regularly override simulation results, add "cheat" keyframes to push a pose past what physics would allow, or run a simulation and then hand-polish the output curves. The most powerful animation pipelines give artists a slider from "fully keyframed" to "fully simulated" — the artist decides how much the simulation influences the final result.

2. Rigid Body Dynamics

2.1 What is a rigid body?

A rigid body is an object that does not deform under force — its shape is fixed. The physics engine tracks each rigid body's state: its position and orientation in world space, its linear velocity (rate of change of position), and its angular velocity (rate of change of orientation). The body also has a mass (resistance to linear acceleration) and a moment of inertia (resistance to angular acceleration, which depends on mass distribution and shape).

Contrast with soft bodies (cloth, skin, jelly) which deform under force and require a different solver, and fluids, which are simulated as large collections of interacting particles or via grid-based methods. This chapter focuses exclusively on rigid bodies — the foundation of all real-time physics in games and interactive 3D.

2.2 Newton's equations of motion

A physics engine runs the following integration loop for every rigid body in the world, every timestep. This is not a simplification — it is exactly what Rapier and PyBullet do internally:

Linear motion:
  F = m × a             (force = mass × acceleration)
  a = F / m             (acceleration from net force)
  v(t+dt) = v(t) + a×dt (velocity Euler integration)
  x(t+dt) = x(t) + v×dt (position Euler integration)

Angular motion:
  τ = I × α             (torque = moment-of-inertia × angular-acceleration)
  ω(t+dt) = ω(t) + α×dt (angular velocity integration)
  θ(t+dt) = θ(t) + ω×dt (orientation integration)

The engine's job is to compute all forces acting on each body — gravity, contact forces from collisions, constraint forces from joints — and integrate them forward. Everything else in the physics pipeline feeds into the force and impulse terms on the right-hand side of these equations.

2.3 The fixed timestep and why it matters

Physics engines use a fixed timestep dt (typically 1/60 s or 1/120 s), independent of the render frame rate. If a frame takes 32 ms (30 fps) and the physics step is 16.67 ms (60 Hz), the engine runs two physics steps per render frame to stay in sync.

The reason is numerical stability. The Euler integration above is only well-behaved for small, consistent dt values. A variable dt proportional to frame time produces different simulation results on different machines and frame rates — a 60 fps machine and a 30 fps machine would produce different collision outcomes from the same initial conditions. A fixed dt makes the simulation deterministic and reproducible: the same input always produces the same output, regardless of hardware.

The standard implementation is the fixed timestep accumulator: accumulate elapsed real time; consume it in fixed physics steps; render with the state from the last completed step.

// ANIMATION CONCEPT: fixed timestep accumulator
// Decouples the physics step rate from the render frame rate.
// accumulator holds "leftover" real time not yet consumed by physics.

const FIXED_DT   = 1 / 60;   // physics step: 60 Hz
let   accumulator = 0;

function animate() {
    requestAnimationFrame(animate);

    const elapsed = clock.getDelta();   // real seconds since last frame
    accumulator  += elapsed;

    // Consume fixed steps — may run 0, 1, or 2 steps per render frame.
    // If the frame is fast (120 fps), accumulator never reaches FIXED_DT
    // and the physics step is skipped — the last physics state is re-rendered.
    while (accumulator >= FIXED_DT) {
        world.step();                   // Rapier advances world by FIXED_DT internally
        accumulator -= FIXED_DT;
    }

    // Sync Three.js mesh positions to Rapier body positions after stepping.
    syncPhysicsToRender();

    renderer.render(scene, camera);
}

2.4 Restitution and friction

When two bodies collide, two material properties govern the outcome. Restitution (coefficient of restitution, e) controls how much kinetic energy is preserved after the collision — it determines bounciness. Friction controls the tangential force that resists sliding between surfaces.

  • e = 0.0 — perfectly inelastic: all kinetic energy absorbed, no bounce (clay, foam)
  • e = 1.0 — perfectly elastic: full kinetic energy preserved (theoretical billiard ball)
  • Real materials lie between: rubber ≈ 0.8, steel on steel ≈ 0.6, wood ≈ 0.3
MaterialTypical restitutionTypical friction
Rubber ball0.7–0.90.8
Steel sphere0.5–0.70.3
Wood box0.2–0.40.5
Clay / foam0.0–0.10.6
Ice0.10.03
Rapier's collision material model. In Rapier, restitution and friction are properties of the Collider, not the RigidBody. When two colliders contact, Rapier combines their material values using a configurable rule (average, minimum, maximum, or multiply). The default is to multiply friction coefficients and average restitution. You can override this per-collider with collider.setRestitutionCombineRule(...) and collider.setFrictionCombineRule(...).

3. Collision Detection

3.1 The two-phase pipeline

A naive collision detection system would test every body against every other body every frame — O(n²) tests. With 100 objects that is 10,000 tests per frame; with 1,000 objects it is 1,000,000. Modern physics engines split the work into two phases to make this tractable:

PhaseGoalAlgorithmOutput
Broad phase Quickly eliminate pairs that cannot possibly collide AABB sweep-and-prune, BVH trees Candidate pairs only
Narrow phase Precisely compute contact points and normals for candidate pairs GJK + EPA, SAT Contact manifold (points, normals, depths)

The broad phase uses Axis-Aligned Bounding Boxes (AABBs) — cheap rectangle-overlap tests that conservatively enclose each object. Only pairs whose AABBs overlap proceed to the narrow phase. Because most objects are far apart most of the time, the broad phase eliminates the vast majority of pairs before the expensive geometry tests begin.

3.2 Collision shapes (colliders)

The collision shape is separate from the render mesh. Engines use simplified convex shapes because they are cheaper to test and always produce a well-defined contact normal. Using the render mesh directly (a triangle mesh collider) is expensive and reserved for static environment geometry that never moves.

ShapeRapier descriptorBest forCost
Ball (sphere)ColliderDesc.ball(radius)Projectiles, marbles, jointsVery cheap
CuboidColliderDesc.cuboid(hx, hy, hz)Boxes, crates, terrain chunksCheap
CapsuleColliderDesc.capsule(halfHeight, radius)Characters, pillars, chain linksCheap
CylinderColliderDesc.cylinder(halfHeight, radius)Barrels, columnsModerate
Convex hullColliderDesc.convexHull(points)Arbitrary convex propsModerate
Triangle meshColliderDesc.trimesh(verts, indices)Static environment onlyExpensive

3.3 Collision events

Rapier can fire events when colliders start or stop overlapping. This is how a game knows a ball hit the ground, a bullet hit a target, or a character landed — and can respond by playing a sound, spawning a particle effect, or updating a score.

// ANIMATION CONCEPT: collision event queue
// Rapier collects events during world.step() into an EventQueue.
// You drain the queue each frame to respond to contact events.
// This decouples physics from game logic cleanly.

const eventQueue = new RAPIER.EventQueue(true);

// In the animation loop, pass the event queue to world.step():
world.step(eventQueue);

// Then drain it — each event carries the two collider handles and
// a boolean indicating whether contact started or ended.
eventQueue.drainCollisionEvents((handle1, handle2, started) => {
    if (started) {
        console.log(`Colliders ${handle1} and ${handle2} started touching`);
        // → play impact sound, spawn dust particle, increment counter, etc.
    }
});
Why not use the render mesh as a collider? A typical character mesh has 5,000–50,000 triangles. Testing all pairs of triangles between two meshes every frame at 60 Hz is prohibitively expensive — O(n×m) narrow-phase tests per pair. Convex hulls reduce this to a single GJK query regardless of triangle count. For static environment geometry (floors, walls) a triangle mesh collider is acceptable because a static body is never tested against another static body — only against dynamic bodies.

4. Rapier + Three.js: Integration Pattern

4.1 Loading Rapier as a WASM module

Rapier is compiled to WebAssembly. It must be initialised asynchronously before the physics world can be created — the RAPIER.init() call downloads and compiles the .wasm binary, which takes a moment on first load.

// STEP 0: Import Rapier from CDN as an ES module.
// rapier3d-compat is the compatibility build — it supports both
// native ES module imports and CommonJS bundler environments.
// Always pin to an exact version; never use @latest in course demos.

import RAPIER from 'https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@dimforge/rapier3d-compat@0.12.0/rapier.es.js';

// STEP 1: Initialise the WASM binary.
// Everything that uses Rapier must run AFTER this resolves.
// Wrap your init code in an async function and await this call.

await RAPIER.init();

// STEP 2: Create the physics world with a gravity vector.
// Gravity is a Vector3 — negative Y means downward in Three.js / Rapier convention.
// Units are SI: metres, kilograms, seconds.
// 9.81 m/s² matches Earth gravity; adjust freely for game feel.

const gravity = { x: 0.0, y: -9.81, z: 0.0 };
const world   = new RAPIER.World(gravity);

4.2 Creating a rigid body and collider

In Rapier, a RigidBody and a Collider are always separate objects. The body holds mass and velocity; the collider defines the shape used for collision detection. You attach one or more colliders to a rigid body after creating both.

// ANIMATION CONCEPT: rigid body + collider are separate objects in Rapier.
// A RigidBodyDesc is a blueprint — it describes the body before creation.
// world.createRigidBody(desc) commits it to the simulation and returns a handle.

// STEP 3: Create a dynamic rigid body.
// .setTranslation() sets the initial world-space position in metres.
const bodyDesc = RAPIER.RigidBodyDesc.dynamic()
    .setTranslation(0.0, 5.0, 0.0);    // start 5 m above the origin
const body = world.createRigidBody(bodyDesc);

// STEP 4: Attach a collider (shape) to the body.
// ColliderDesc.cuboid(hx, hy, hz) → a box with half-extents in metres.
// A 1×1×1 m box has half-extents of 0.5 on each axis.
// .setDensity()     → mass is computed automatically from volume × density
// .setRestitution() → bounciness (0 = no bounce, 1 = full bounce)
// .setFriction()    → surface grip (0 = frictionless, 1 = very grippy)

const colliderDesc = RAPIER.ColliderDesc.cuboid(0.5, 0.5, 0.5)
    .setDensity(1.0)
    .setRestitution(0.3)
    .setFriction(0.7);
world.createCollider(colliderDesc, body);

// STEP 5: Create a matching Three.js mesh — purely visual.
// Rapier owns the physics position; Three.js is the display layer.
// The mesh dimensions must match the collider dimensions exactly.

const geometry = new THREE.BoxGeometry(1, 1, 1);
const material = new THREE.MeshStandardMaterial({ color: 0x4f9cf9 });
const mesh     = new THREE.Mesh(geometry, material);
scene.add(mesh);

// STEP 6: Store the body/mesh pair so we can sync them every frame.
const bodies = [{ body, mesh }];

4.3 The sync function — copying physics state to the render layer

// ANIMATION CONCEPT: physics bodies and render meshes are separate objects.
// Rapier owns the ground-truth position and orientation.
// Three.js is the display layer — it only knows what we tell it.
// Every frame, after world.step(), copy Rapier state → Three.js transforms.

function syncPhysicsToRender() {
    for (const { body, mesh } of bodies) {
        const pos = body.translation();   // {x, y, z} in world space (metres)
        const rot = body.rotation();      // {x, y, z, w} quaternion

        mesh.position.set(pos.x, pos.y, pos.z);
        mesh.quaternion.set(rot.x, rot.y, rot.z, rot.w);
    }
}

4.4 Static bodies and the ground plane

// ANIMATION CONCEPT: static bodies do not move and are not affected by forces.
// Use RigidBodyDesc.fixed() for floors, walls, and terrain.
// Rapier skips expensive integration and sleep/wake logic for statics.
// A static body still participates in collision detection — dynamic bodies bounce off it.

const groundDesc     = RAPIER.RigidBodyDesc.fixed();
const groundBody     = world.createRigidBody(groundDesc);
const groundCollider = RAPIER.ColliderDesc.cuboid(50.0, 0.1, 50.0)
    .setFriction(0.5);
world.createCollider(groundCollider, groundBody);

// Matching visual ground mesh in Three.js (must match collider dimensions)
const groundMesh = new THREE.Mesh(
    new THREE.BoxGeometry(100, 0.2, 100),
    new THREE.MeshStandardMaterial({ color: 0x252b38 })
);
scene.add(groundMesh);
Rapier units are SI. Distance is in metres, time in seconds, mass in kilograms. If your Three.js scene uses a different scale (e.g. 1 unit = 1 cm), apply a uniform scale factor when converting positions between the two. Using SI units directly in both Rapier and Three.js — as in all course demos — is the recommended approach: gravity at 9.81 m/s², a falling box 1 m on each side, dropped from 5 m. The numbers feel physically right immediately.

5. Constraints and Joints

5.1 What is a constraint?

A constraint removes degrees of freedom from a rigid body or a pair of bodies. Without constraints, a rigid body in 3D has 6 DOF: 3 translational (x, y, z) and 3 rotational (pitch, yaw, roll). A joint constrains some of these DOF while leaving others free.

The constraint solver is one of the most computationally complex parts of a physics engine. At each timestep, Rapier formulates a linear system whose solution gives the constraint impulses — the forces that must be applied to each body this frame to satisfy all active constraints simultaneously. Understanding this is not required to use joints, but it explains why adding many interconnected constraints can slow the simulation.

5.2 Joint types in Rapier

Joint typeRapier descriptorDOF removedTypical use
FixedJointData.fixed(...)All 6Weld two bodies permanently together
Revolute (hinge)JointData.revolute(...)5 (1 rotation axis free)Door hinge, elbow, wheel axle
Ball-socket (spherical)JointData.spherical(...)3 (all rotations free)Shoulder, hip, ragdoll connection
PrismaticJointData.prismatic(...)5 (1 translation axis free)Piston, sliding drawer, elevator

5.3 Building a chain (rope or ragdoll arm)

A chain is an array of rigid bodies where each body is connected to the next by a revolute joint. The joints are defined by anchor points on each body in their local coordinate space. The joint constrains those two anchor points to remain coincident while permitting rotation around a specified axis.

// ANIMATION CONCEPT: a chain is an array of rigid bodies connected by joints.
// Each joint is defined by two anchors — one per body — in local space.
// Rapier keeps the anchors coincident by applying corrective impulses every step.
// The result is a physically simulated rope or arm with no keyframing.

const LINK_COUNT  = 8;
const LINK_LENGTH = 0.5;   // metres per link
let   prevBody    = null;

for (let i = 0; i < LINK_COUNT; i++) {
    const y = 4.0 - i * LINK_LENGTH;

    // The first link is fixed (the anchor point for the whole chain).
    // All subsequent links are dynamic and fall under gravity.
    const bodyDesc = i === 0
        ? RAPIER.RigidBodyDesc.fixed().setTranslation(0, y, 0)
        : RAPIER.RigidBodyDesc.dynamic().setTranslation(0, y, 0);

    const body = world.createRigidBody(bodyDesc);

    // Capsule collider matches the visual link: a rounded cylinder.
    world.createCollider(
        RAPIER.ColliderDesc.capsule(LINK_LENGTH * 0.4, 0.08),
        body
    );

    if (prevBody) {
        // Revolute joint: rotates around the Z axis only.
        // anchor1 → bottom of the previous link (in its local space)
        // anchor2 → top of this link (in its local space)
        // axis    → the rotation axis (Z = out of screen in XY view)
        const joint = RAPIER.JointData.revolute(
            { x: 0, y: -LINK_LENGTH * 0.5, z: 0 },  // anchor on prevBody
            { x: 0, y:  LINK_LENGTH * 0.5, z: 0 },  // anchor on body
            { x: 0, y: 0, z: 1 }                     // rotation axis
        );
        // true → wake bodies from sleep when the joint is created
        world.createImpulseJoint(joint, prevBody, body, true);
    }
    prevBody = body;
}
Ragdolls are a network of joints. A game character ragdoll is exactly this pattern at scale: 15–20 rigid bodies (torso, head, upper arms, forearms, hands, pelvis, thighs, shins, feet) connected by ball-socket and revolute joints with angular limits. When a character dies, the game switches from the keyframed animation to the ragdoll simulation: the bodies are initialised to the character's current animated pose and velocity, then handed to the physics engine. The result — bodies tumbling with physically plausible follow-through — is immediately recognisable from any action game. The SkinnedMesh bone API from seminar 04 is what the physics engine drives: bone.position and bone.quaternion are set by rigid body positions, not by the AnimationMixer.

6. Spring-Damper Systems and Secondary Animation

6.1 What is secondary animation?

Secondary animation is motion that is caused by primary motion, not explicitly authored. When a character's body moves suddenly, secondary elements — hair, a cape, dangling accessories, soft body parts — should react with follow-through (continuing to move after the primary motion stops) and overshoot (moving past the resting position, then settling back). These are two of the twelve principles of animation described by Disney animators Ollie Johnston and Frank Thomas in The Illusion of Life (1981). Physics-based secondary animation produces these behaviours automatically, without any additional keyframing.

6.2 The spring-damper equation

The spring-mass-damper model is the fundamental tool for secondary animation. It models a mass attached to a spring with a damping force that resists velocity. The spring pulls the mass toward a target (the rest position, which the primary animation drives); the damper bleeds off energy to prevent eternal oscillation.

F = -k × (x - x_rest)  -  d × v

Where:
  x       — current position of the secondary object
  x_rest  — rest/target position (driven by the primary animation)
  v       — current velocity of the secondary object
  k       — spring stiffness (N/m): higher = snappier, faster settling
  d       — damping coefficient: higher = less oscillation, faster settling
  F       — net force applied to the secondary object this frame

Integration (semi-implicit Euler — more stable than explicit Euler):
  a           = F / mass
  v(t+dt)     = v(t) + a × dt
  x(t+dt)     = x(t) + v(t+dt) × dt   // use the new velocity, not the old one

6.3 Stiffness, damping and their visual effect

Stiffness kDamping dVisual feel
HighHighStiff and snappy, almost rigid — good for a helmet or shield
HighLowStiff but oscillates vigorously — like a metal spring or antenna
LowHighSlow, heavy follow-through, no oscillation — good for a heavy coat
LowLowSlow oscillation that takes a long time to settle — like jelly

6.4 Critical damping

Critical damping is the damping value at which the system returns to equilibrium as fast as possible without oscillating. It is the boundary between underdamped (oscillates) and overdamped (slow return).

Critical damping condition:
  d_crit = 2 × sqrt(k × mass)

Underdamped   (d < d_crit): system oscillates — overshoots and rings
Critically damped (d = d_crit): fastest return to rest, no overshoot
Overdamped    (d > d_crit): slow, sluggish return to rest, no overshoot

Professional animators typically use slightly underdamped springs (d ≈ 0.5 × d_crit) because a small amount of overshoot reads as natural and lively. A perfectly critically damped spring can look mechanical. Demo 4 exposes stiffness and damping sliders so you can find this sweet spot interactively.

Secondary animation in professional production. Every major game engine exposes a spring-damper system: Unreal's Spring Arm Component (camera rigs), Unity's Spring Joint, and the jiggle bone concept in proprietary character rigs. Character TDs attach jiggle bones to flesh, hair, tails, ears, and accessory joints — the spring-damper code runs every frame, automatically producing follow-through and overshoot that would take hours to keyframe manually. Demo 4 implements this pattern from first principles, in plain JavaScript, with no physics engine required.

7. PyBullet + OpenGL: Domino Cascade

7.1 What is PyBullet?

PyBullet is the Python binding for the Bullet Physics SDK — a production-proven open-source physics engine used in blockbuster films (Guardians of the Galaxy, Batman v Superman) and game engines (Godot's older physics backend). Today PyBullet is widely used in robotics research and reinforcement learning (OpenAI Gym, DeepMind Control Suite) because it is accurate, scriptable, and fast enough for simulation-at-scale workloads.

The key distinction from Rapier: PyBullet runs entirely on the CPU, in Python, with no GPU or WASM involvement. It owns the physics world exclusively — collision detection, constraint solving, integration — while a separate renderer reads the resulting body poses each frame and draws the scene. This physics/render split is the architectural pattern that Demo 5 teaches.

7.2 Installation

Demo 5 is managed with uv, which handles all dependencies in an isolated virtual environment. No manual pip install is required.

uv sync --extra arm64   # resolves and installs all dependencies
uv run python 05_ca_pybullet_domino.py

The demo depends on PyBullet (physics), PySide6 (Qt application and OpenGL widget), PyOpenGL (raw OpenGL 3.3 calls), and NumPy (matrix math). It runs as a full desktop GUI application and requires a local machine with a display — it is not designed for headless environments such as Codespaces or Colab.

PyBullet is used in DIRECT mode even though a window is open. The Qt/OpenGL renderer provides the visible window — PyBullet never opens one. pybullet.connect(pybullet.DIRECT) runs the physics engine in headless mode; the renderer reads poses via getBasePositionAndOrientation() and draws them independently. This is intentional: neither subsystem knows the internals of the other, making it straightforward to swap either one without touching the other.

7.4 The physics/render split

The central design pattern of Demo 5 is a clean separation between two independent subsystems: DominoPhysics (owns the PyBullet world) and DominoViewport (owns the OpenGL renderer). They communicate through one narrow interface: a list of (pos, orn) tuples that DominoPhysics writes after every step and DominoViewport reads every frame.

# ANIMATION CONCEPT: physics and rendering are independent subsystems.
# DominoPhysics exposes only three public arrays to the renderer.
# The renderer never calls any PyBullet function — it only reads these arrays.

class DominoPhysics:
    body_states:   list[tuple]   # [(pos, orn), …] — updated after every step()
    platform_data: list[tuple]   # static box poses — set once during connect()
    body_colors:   list[tuple]   # [(r, g, b), …]  — set once, never changes

    def step(self, n: int) -> None:
        # Advance the simulation by n physics steps (fixed dt = 1/240 s each).
        for _ in range(n):
            pybullet.stepSimulation()
        self._sync_states()      # copy PyBullet poses → body_states

    def _sync_states(self) -> None:
        self.body_states = [
            pybullet.getBasePositionAndOrientation(bid)
            for bid in self._dom_ids
        ]

# DominoViewport reads body_states in paintGL() and builds a 4×4 model
# matrix for each body — no PyBullet calls, no physics knowledge required.
# Swapping PyBullet for a different physics engine only changes DominoPhysics.

The Qt QTimer fires at approximately 60 Hz. Each tick calls physics.step(n) (advancing the simulation by n substeps) and then schedules a repaint. At the default 1× speed, n = 4 substeps × 1/240 s = 16.7 ms of simulated time per tick, matching wall-clock time. Increasing n compresses simulated time without changing physics accuracy — the same number of solver iterations runs per step.

7.5 Domino layout: procedural scene generation

All domino positions are computed procedurally at startup by build_layout(), which assembles the cascade from a sequence of primitive layout operations. No coordinates are hard-coded — the entire scene is derived from a small set of physical constants.

_run(ox, oy, oz, yaw, n, sp)n dominoes in a straight line at constant elevationsp — centre-to-centre spacing
_stair(ox, oy, oz, yaw, n, rise, run)n dominoes stepping up or down by rise metres per step; also generates static platform boxes for elevated tilesrise — negative for descent, positive for ascent

The cascade runs through five acts in sequence. Only domino 0 ever receives an external force — every subsequent fall is caused by physical contact between neighbours:

# ANIMATION CONCEPT: the cascade is a chain of physical contacts, not keyframes.
# build_layout() returns an ordered list of DP (domino placement) objects.
# Each DP holds (x, y, z, yaw) — world-space position and heading direction.

# Act 1 — Entry platform: flat elevated run, heading +X.
#   START_H = DESCEND_STEPS × STEP_RISE = 10 × 0.05 = 0.50 m
entry, plats   = _stair(0.0, 0.0, START_H, yaw=0.0, n=8, rise=0.0)

# Act 2 — Descent: stair-stepped ramp from START_H down to z=0.
#   Each step drops by STEP_RISE = 0.05 m; spacing is wider (0.165 m) because
#   gravity accelerates the falling tile forward on the downhill run.
descend, plats = _stair(…, n=10, rise=-STEP_RISE, run=DESCEND_SPACING)

# Act 3 — Ground run: flat section at z=0 on the global ground plane.
#   No platform boxes are generated here — the plane.urdf provides the surface.
floor_run, _   = _stair(…, n=6, rise=0.0)

# Act 4 — Ascent: stair-stepped ramp from z=0 up to PLATEAU_H.
#   Steps are shallower (rise=0.030 m) and spacing tighter (0.130 m) so the
#   kinetic energy from the descent is enough to carry the wave up.
#   This is the same energy exchange as a skate half-pipe.
ascend, plats  = _stair(…, n=8, rise=A_RISE, run=ASCEND_SPACING)

# Act 5 — Plateau: flat section at PLATEAU_H leading to the fork domino.
plateau, plats = _stair(…, n=5, rise=0.0)

# Fork: the last plateau domino falls toward two neighbours placed at ±35°.
# Each neighbour starts its own arm — the cascade wave splits.
for sign in (+1.0, -1.0):
    arm_yaw = pf.yaw + sign * math.radians(35.0)
    arm, plats = _stair(ax, ay, PLATEAU_H, arm_yaw, n=12, rise=0.0)

Elevated dominoes need a solid surface under them. _stair() automatically generates a static platform box for every step above z=0 — a thin slab whose top face aligns exactly with the domino's footing elevation. The ground plane (plane.urdf) supports all dominoes at z=0; no box is generated for those.

Energy conservation in the cascade. The descent and ascent ramps demonstrate the conversion between gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy — the same principle as a skate half-pipe. On the way down, each falling tile accelerates because gravity adds energy at every step; this is why the descending-ramp spacing (DESCEND_SPACING = 0.165 m) is wider than on flat ground. On the ascent, the tile decelerates as kinetic energy converts back to potential energy; spacing is tighter (ASCEND_SPACING = 0.130 m) to keep each tile within reach of its neighbour before momentum runs out. The ascending step rise (A_RISE = 0.030 m) is kept smaller than the descending step (STEP_RISE = 0.050 m) so the total height gained on the way up is less than the height lost on the way down — the cascade always has enough energy to complete the climb.

7.6 Starting the cascade: angular impulse

When the user clicks Start, a single external torque is applied to domino 0 for one physics step. A torque (angular impulse) is used rather than a linear force because the goal is to tip the tile — rotate it forward about its base edge — not slide it. Once the tile tilts past its tipping angle (arctan(depth / height) ≈ 5.7°), its centre of mass passes beyond the pivot edge and gravity takes over. Every subsequent domino falls because it is physically struck by the one before it.

# ANIMATION CONCEPT: angular impulse to start the cascade.
# The torque axis is perpendicular to the domino's heading direction
# in the XY plane, so the tile rotates forward rather than sideways.
# A vector perpendicular to (cos θ, sin θ, 0) is (-sin θ, cos θ, 0).

p0 = self._placements[0]
pybullet.applyExternalTorque(
    self._dom_ids[0], -1,
    torqueObj=[-math.sin(p0.yaw) * 8.0,
                math.cos(p0.yaw) * 8.0,
                0.0],
    flags=pybullet.WORLD_FRAME,
)

7.7 OpenGL renderer: from quaternion to screen

The renderer (DominoViewport) is a QOpenGLWidget that uses OpenGL 3.3 Core Profile. All geometry is drawn with the same unit-cube VAO — only the model matrix, colour, and specular exponent change between draw calls. The rendering pipeline per frame is:

  1. Build the model matrix from the body's physics pose. The PyBullet quaternion (x, y, z, w) is expanded to a 3×3 rotation matrix, then embedded in a 4×4 matrix with the translation and scale: M = T × R × S.
  2. Upload uniforms — the model matrix, base colour, and specular power — to the GLSL shader via glUniformMatrix4fv and glUniform4f.
  3. Draw the body in its gradient colour (blue → cyan → green → gold → red across the sequence).
  4. Draw the face stripe — a thin ivory box placed on the domino's front face. Its centre is offset along the body's local X axis, extracted from column 0 of the model matrix: this works correctly for any orientation, including mid-fall.
# ANIMATION CONCEPT: per-body model matrix from physics pose.
# _build_model_mat() converts a PyBullet (pos, orn) pair into a 4×4 matrix
# that the GLSL vertex shader uses to transform object-space vertices
# into world space: clip_pos = Projection × View × Model × vertex_pos.

def _build_model_mat(pos, orn, sx, sy, sz) -> np.ndarray:
    T = np.eye(4, dtype=np.float32)
    T[0,3], T[1,3], T[2,3] = pos   # translation
    R = _quat_to_mat4(orn)          # rotation from (x, y, z, w) quaternion
    S = np.diag([sx, sy, sz, 1.0])  # non-uniform scale (domino dimensions)
    return T @ R @ S                 # T × R × S: scale first, rotate, translate

# Face stripe: offset along the domino's local X axis (column 0 of M).
# Dividing by DOM_D gives the unit direction in world space.
local_x  = m[:3, 0] / DOM_D
face_pos = np.array(pos) + local_x * (DOM_D * 0.5 + FACE_D * 0.5)

The vertex shader passes world-space position and normal to the fragment shader, which implements Blinn-Phong shading with two lights (a warm key light and a cool fill light at 35% intensity) and applies gamma correction (γ = 1/2.2) before writing to the framebuffer. The floor uses a procedural checkerboard computed from world-space XY coordinates — no texture file required.

7.8 Interactive camera

The InteractiveCamera class maintains the view in spherical coordinates around a target point: yaw (horizontal angle), pitch (vertical angle), and distance. The eye position is derived each frame from these three values, keeping the camera always looking at the target.

PyBullet vs Rapier: choosing the right tool. Use Rapier in the browser when you need real-time interactive physics in a web app alongside Three.js — it is designed for that use case. Use PyBullet in Python when the simulation is the centrepiece: a complex procedurally generated scene, physics parameters you want to explore programmatically, or a renderer you control at the OpenGL level. Demo 5 sits at the far end of that spectrum — PyBullet computes motion, a custom OpenGL 3.3 pipeline renders it, and a Qt application ties them together. Neither subsystem depends on the other's internals.
