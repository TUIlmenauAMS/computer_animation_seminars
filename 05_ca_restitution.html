Demo 2: Bounciness Explorer

A 5 × 5 grid of balls drops simultaneously from the same height. Each column has a different restitution coefficient (0.0 → 1.0 in 0.25 steps) and each row a different friction coefficient (0.05 → 0.9). Watch the grid settle: the leftmost column lies flat while the rightmost is still bouncing. Labels above each ball show its exact restitution value.

Use the sliders to change gravity and drop height, then press Drop to re-run the experiment. Read the source as a tutorial — every STEP block maps a Rapier concept to the matching Three.js code.

ANIMATION CONCEPT — Restitution: the coefficient of elasticity
Restitution e (0.0–1.0) controls how much kinetic energy is preserved after a collision. At e = 0 (perfectly inelastic) no energy bounces back — the ball stops dead. At e = 1 (perfectly elastic) all energy is preserved and the ball bounces back to its original drop height indefinitely. Real materials sit between: rubber ≈ 0.8, wood ≈ 0.3, clay ≈ 0.05. In Rapier, restitution is set on the ColliderDesc, not the RigidBodyDesc — it is a surface property, not a mass property.
RAPIER CONCEPT — Combine rules for restitution and friction
When two colliders meet, Rapier must combine their individual values into one contact value. The default rule for restitution is average: the ground here has e = 0.0, so the effective restitution for a ball with e = 1.0 is (1.0 + 0.0) / 2 = 0.5. To make the ball's value authoritative, set the ground to e = 1.0 and use setRestitutionCombineRule(RAPIER.CoefficientCombineRule.Min) — or set the ground's rule to Multiply. This demo uses the Max rule on each ball's collider so its restitution dominates the contact, making the grid's variation clean and direct to observe.
ANIMATION CONCEPT — Sphere colliders vs box colliders
This demo uses ColliderDesc.ball(radius) — a sphere shape. Spheres are the cheapest possible collider: Rapier resolves sphere–plane contact in a single distance check, with no edge or corner ambiguity. This is why billard balls, marbles, and projectiles are almost always spheres in physics simulations even when their visual mesh is not. The Three.js render mesh is a SphereGeometry sized to match.
